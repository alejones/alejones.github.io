# Will and attention 

I listen to a lot of podcasts. Probably to many. Often in conversation I'll have some fact that I learned from one. Usually I don't keep coming back to an episode and thinking about it. A recent-ish episode of Search Engine has stuck with me. 

I'd highly reccomend you give [Unreliable Narrator](https://www.searchengine.show/unreliable-narrator) a listen. I should buy the [book](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/778492/will-and-attention-by-meghan-ogieblyn/) from the guest Meghan O’Gieblyn.

## What the episode is about
Disclaimer: I haven't re-listened to this episode. You are getting my recollection from my poor memory.

Meghan O’Gieblyn speaks about her addiction and what it has taken for her to get to the healthier place she is at now. One of the things she talks about is the difference between will and attention.

If someone overcomes something hard, people will often say that person is strong willed. They had the fortitude to overcome a challenge. The have some internal drive that made them unstoppable. 

Meghan argues that overcoming a challenge, in her case addiction, is less about the amount of will power you may or may not have, but about the attention you pay to all the small daily challenges.

## How does this apply to swimming
I have been coaching for 15 years. I think the entire time people have told me, some version of "Kids these days". I find it exausting. It's rarely followed by something helpful. It's an easy out when things aren't going how you want. 

Lane 2 can't read the pace clock and never leaves on time. Kids these days
Calvin is never on time. Kids these days
All Suzie worries about is moving up to a new group. She is barely middle of the pack in this group. Kids these days.

Sometimes these are valid, but it's usually not kids. It's the kid. I don't hear how that coach is trying to structure things to get the result they are looking for. 

Do you call out the kids who are on time? do you let a lane fail and see the consiquences of not reading a pace clock? Do you make it clear you think they can do it? I know there are kids that this won't work for. But I sure hope we can do more to try.

Ultimately, I think the "cure" to all of this is making them care. It's not that all the kids are so weak willed and brain rotted that they can't read a pace clock, I promise they can, it's they are paying attention to something else in practice. What is the joke I want to tell, how can I get someone to pay attention to me, how long is left, how many yards are in this set?

Do you sell your program? Are you teaching something they want to learn? Do kids know why they are doing what they are doing (and future rant, do you know why you are doing what you are doing?)?

Do you make you practices/group/program special? Could a kid tell you what that is? Can they look at another group and only see what they are missing, or can they see what they would be losing if they swiched to a new practice/group/program.

If athletes care about how many strokes they are taking, they may count with out you asking. Do you talk about why being efficent is important? 

### Why have I been thinking about will and attention?

I'll admit, my favorite kids are the ones who come to practice and when I (metaphorically) say jump, they ask how high. I  thought these were the most motivated, easyist to coach, and in this context, strongest willed. Without thinking about it, they were fully present.

I think switching the framing to all kids can be more like this if they are willing to pay attention is exciting. I have always tried to be a teaching who tries to teach myself out of a job. I usually start a piece of feedback with a question, "how did it feel when you were doing x?" or "where were your feet on your turn?". I want to start incorperating more questions about what athletes were thinking about. Can you pay attention to what you are paying attention to? 

I want to use this as feedback, are they thinking about what I'm teaching. Am I teaching things they care about learning?
