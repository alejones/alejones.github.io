# Will and attention 

I listen to a lot of podcasts. Probably too many. Often in conversation I'll have some fact that I learned from one. Usually I don't keep coming back to an episode and thinking about it. A recent-ish episode of Search Engine has stuck with me. 

I'd highly recommend you give [Unreliable Narrator](https://www.searchengine.show/unreliable-narrator) a listen. I should buy the [book](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/778492/will-and-attention-by-meghan-ogieblyn/) from the guest Meghan O’Gieblyn.

## What the episode is about
Disclaimer: I haven't re-listened to this episode. You are getting my recollection from my poor memory.

Meghan O’Gieblyn speaks about her addiction and what it has taken for her to get to the healthier place she is at now. One of the things she talks about is the difference between will and attention.

If someone overcomes something hard, people will often say that person is strong willed. They have the fortitude to overcome a challenge. They have some internal drive that makes them unstoppable. 

Meghan argues that overcoming a challenge, in her case addiction, is less about the amount of will power you may or may not have, but about the attention you pay to all the small daily challenges. 

I'm going to listen again. I really think it's worth your time as well. If my memory is way off, send me an email and tell me. I would love to talk about it.

## How does this apply to swimming
I have been coaching for 15 years. I think the entire time people have told me, some version of "Kids these days". I find it exhausting. It's rarely followed by something helpful. It's an easy out when things aren't going how you want. 

Lane 2 can't read the pace clock and never leaves on time. Kids these days.
Calvin is never in the water time. Kids these days.
All Suzie worries about is moving up to a new group. She is barely middle of the pack in this group. Kids these days.

Sometimes these are valid, but it's usually not kids. It's the kid. I don't hear how that coach is trying to structure things to get the result they are looking for. 

Do you call out the kids who are on time? Does Calvin know you are annoyed he isn't in the water, and he doesn't care anymore? Or do you get excited when Calvin is on time for the first time this month? Do you let a lane fail and see the consequences of not reading a pace clock? Do they care they don't understand what's happening? Do you make it clear you think they can do it? I know there are kids that this won't work for. But I have seen a lot of coaches that could do more.

Ultimately, I think the "cure" to all of this is making them care. It's not that all the kids are so weak willed and brain rotted that they can't read a pace clock, I promise they can, I think they are paying attention to something else in practice. What is the joke I want to tell, how can I get someone to pay attention to me, how long until I go home, how many yards are in this practice?

Do you sell your program? Are you teaching something they want to learn? Do kids know why they are doing what they are doing (and future rant, do you know why you are doing what you are doing?)?

Do you make your practice/group/program special? Could a kid tell you why it is? Do they look at another group and only see what they are missing, or can they see what they would be losing if they switched to a new practice/group/program?

If athletes care about how many strokes they are taking, they will count without you asking. Do you talk about why being efficient is important? Do think being fast is all that is important?

### Why have I been thinking about will and attention?

I'll admit, my favorite kids are the ones who come to practice and when I (metaphorically) say jump, they ask how high. I think these are the most motivated, easiest to coach, and in this context, strongest willed. Without thinking about it, they are fully present.

I want to make more kids like this, if they are ~~willing~~ wanting to pay attention is exciting. I have always tried to be a teacher who tries to teach myself out of a job. I usually start a piece of feedback with a question, "how did it feel when you were doing x?" or "where were your feet on your turn?". I want to start incorporating more questions about what athletes are thinking about. Can you pay attention to what you are paying attention to? 

I want to use this as feedback: are they thinking about what I'm teaching? Am I teaching things they care about learning?
